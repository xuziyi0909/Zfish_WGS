The ideas

Why we compare a mutant with a control, the handful of words you need, and why a recessive mutation leaves a visible footprint in the genome.

This is the whole job in one picture. Any two zebrafish differ from the reference genome at millions of places; lab strains are very diverse. Almost all of that is harmless background. The pipeline removes what the sibling also carries, keeps changes that hit proteins, and ranks them. Even then we are left with hundreds of candidates, so the last steps are always biology and bench work: which gene fits the phenotype, and does the variant segregate with it.

Key words

Seven words cover most of the report

WordWhat it means
ReadOne short piece of DNA from the sequencer (here 151 letters long)
CoverageHow many reads cover each position on average: 15× is good, 3× is thin
Reference genomeThe standard zebrafish sequence (GRCz11) that every fish is compared with
VariantA difference from the reference: a changed letter (SNV) or a small insertion or deletion (indel)
HomozygousBoth copies of the chromosome carry the variant; heterozygous: only one copy
Allele fractionShare of reads showing the variant: about 1.0 if homozygous, about 0.5 if heterozygous
ImpactPredicted effect: HIGH breaks the protein (stop, frameshift, splice site); MODERATE swaps one amino acid

Zebrafish WGS tutorial · 5

These seven words are enough to read the report. Coverage is the one people underestimate: at 3x, a heterozygous variant is often seen in only one read, or none, so a single fish at low coverage can look homozygous by chance. Allele fraction is what sibling mode uses: it is simply the share of reads at that spot that carry the variant.

Recessive genetics

Wild-type-looking siblings can carry the mutation

Two carriers (+/m × +/m) give mutants and siblings. Share of reads that show the mutation:

So a sibling control can show the mutation in some reads. Requiring it to be completely absent can throw the real mutation away. That is why the pipeline has a sibling mode.

Zebrafish WGS tutorial · 6

This is the most important genetic point in the tutorial. Siblings are the best controls because they share most of the background with the mutant. But two thirds of wild-type-looking siblings carry one copy of a recessive mutation. A carrier shows the mutation in about half its reads; a pool of siblings in about a third. If the analysis demanded zero reads in the control, it would discard the causal mutation two times out of three. What a sibling can never be is homozygous for it, because then it would be a mutant.

Around the mutation, the mutant inherited the same stretch of chromosome twice, so every variant there is homozygous.

The pipeline counts homozygous mutant variants per Mb. A tall peak shows where to look for the mutation.

Each colour is a stretch of chromosome inherited from one grandparent · 7

A mutation is never inherited alone. It travels with the piece of chromosome it sits on, and recombination only slowly breaks that piece up over generations. So in a homozygous mutant, the region around the mutation is identical on both copies, and every ordinary variant in that region also looks homozygous. A sibling carries the region on at most one copy. This is homozygosity mapping: the pipeline counts homozygous variants per megabase along the genome, and the tallest peak is where the mutation most likely sits. With few meioses the region can be several megabases long and contain dozens of genes.

This is the one setting that depends on your experiment. Absent mode is strict: the control must show no trace of the variant. That is right for an unrelated control, or for a dominant or brand-new mutation. Sibling mode is for the usual screen design: mutants versus their wild-type-looking siblings. It requires the mutant to carry the variant on both copies, in at least 90 percent of reads, and allows the sibling up to 60 percent, which covers carriers. For a mutant pool with a few mis-sorted fish, lower the 0.9 to 0.8. Changing the mode and re-running only repeats the last steps, about 20 minutes.

Step one cleans each read. Library preparation adds a short synthetic adapter to both ends of every DNA fragment. If the fragment is shorter than the read length, 151 letters here, the sequencer runs past the zebrafish DNA into the adapter, and those letters would not match the genome. fastp finds and cuts them. It also removes poly-G tails, an artefact of two-colour instruments such as the NovaSeq, and drops reads that are mostly low quality or end up shorter than 50 letters. In our data most fragments were short, so most reads were trimmed, but almost all were kept.

Step two places every read on the reference genome with bwa-mem2. Each placement comes with a mapping quality, a measure of how sure the aligner is. A read from a repeat could belong to several places, so it scores low; the variant caller ignores reads below 20. Step three marks duplicates: during library PCR one DNA fragment can be copied many times, and the copies all start at the same position. Only one is counted, so a PCR error cannot masquerade as a variant supported by many reads. After both steps, usable coverage is what is left: 2.8-fold and 3.4-fold here.

Step four finds the differences. To use all the cores, the genome is cut into 30 pieces processed in parallel. For each position, reads are stacked up, ignoring poorly placed reads and low-quality letters. Then all fish are genotyped together, which is important: for every variant we get the read counts in every fish, including fish that do not carry it. That is how we know the sibling's six reads at the tmppe position all show C. Finally the calls are tidied so that each line holds one change and insertions and deletions are written in a standard way, which the known-variant matching needs, and calls below quality 30 are dropped.

Known variants are differences already seen in other zebrafish lines; they are ordinary strain polymorphisms. A mutation from an ENU screen, or a spontaneous one, is essentially always new. The pipeline downloads Ensembl's list of eighteen million known zebrafish variants once and labels every call that matches by position and by the changed letter. In our mutant more than half of the strongest protein-changing candidates were known. The best example is slc39a13: the mouse knockout has a small jaw, so it looked perfect, but it is a catalogued polymorphism.

Matching sounds trivial but has two traps. First, an insertion or deletion inside a repeat, such as one extra A in a run of A's, can be written at several equivalent positions. Our caller always writes it at the leftmost one, but the Ensembl list often does not; without rewriting, 470,839 known indels would never match. Second, a position can carry several different changes. We require the same change, not just the same position, because a new ENU allele can arise at a site that is already variable in other strains, and it should still count as novel.

This table is the exact rule set. Two definitions matter: the mutant has a variant when at least two reads show it and the position has at least three reads; a control is covered when it has at least four reads there. In absent mode, any trace in the control makes a variant shared. In sibling mode the mutant must also show the variant in at least 90 percent of its reads, and the control may show it in up to 60 percent: zero control reads gives mutant-specific, a few gives mutant-enriched. Not homozygous is a sibling-mode bin for variants the mutant carries on only one copy: they cannot be a recessive mutation. With several mutants, mutant_rule decides whether one or all must carry it.

This is the heart of the comparison. For each variant we look at the share of reads that carry it. A recessive mutation must be on both copies in the mutant, so nearly all reads: the orange zone, 90 percent or more. A sibling may be a carrier, about half its reads, or a pool, about a third: the teal zone up to 60 percent. If the sibling has zero reads with good coverage, the variant is mutant-specific; if it has a few, mutant-enriched. Both count as high confidence. Low confidence means the sibling simply had too few reads to judge, which is common at 3x coverage: two million variants fell there in our data. Anything heterozygous in the mutant, or that looks homozygous in the sibling, cannot be the recessive mutation.

This check came straight from a mistake we nearly made. aldh1a2 looked like a great jaw candidate with three missense changes. But the reference genome itself carries rare residues there; published zebrafish cDNAs have exactly what our mutant has. So the mutant is normal and the reference is odd. The pipeline now lines up every missense change with RefSeq proteins, which are built from cDNAs of other fish, and with the same protein in six other vertebrates. If the mutant residue is in a zebrafish RefSeq protein, or is what most vertebrates have, it is moved to priority 5. If the reference residue is identical in every species, the change is more likely to matter, and it is listed first within its priority.

Here is the method behind the aldh1a2 example. For each missense change the pipeline takes the zebrafish protein of the annotated transcript and checks that the reference amino acid really sits at that position. It aligns that protein with up to four NCBI RefSeq proteins of the same gene, preferring curated ones, and with the closest Ensembl Compara ortholog in each of six species: human, rat, chicken, frog, medaka and fugu. It reads the residue lined up with the variant, but only trusts it when the surrounding twenty residues align reasonably well, so a poorly aligned region cannot give a false answer. The rules are applied in this order. Only the first two move a variant to priority 5; conserved changes are listed first within their priority.

Ranking

Every protein-changing variant gets a priority

PriorityRuleIn our data
1HIGH impact, high confidence, homozygous in the mutant21

Priority ignores location. The causal change inside the homozygosity peak may be “only” priority 2, while a priority 1 far away is background. Weigh both.

Priority 0 = not protein-changing (kept in the full table) · 30

Each HIGH or MODERATE variant gets a priority from 1 to 5. One is the classic recessive loss of function: a broken protein on both copies. Two covers strong but less complete evidence, and in a sibling-mode run most missense candidates land here, because sibling mode already requires homozygosity; that is why priority 3 is nearly empty. Four is low confidence. Five is everything the checks flagged: it stays in the tables with the reason written out, but should not be your first PCR. Most important: priority is scored one variant at a time. Always look at where the homozygosity peak is as well.

Warning sign: dozens of variants packed together usually means a repeat or mapping problem · 36

No single number decides. A variant becomes believable when several things line up: good coverage in both samples, several supporting reads, novel, no warnings, a conserved residue, inside the peak, and a gene whose expression or known phenotype fits. Always look at the top few in IGV before ordering primers; it takes two minutes. Real variants show the same changed letter in many independent reads from both strands. The final test is genetic: a causal recessive mutation is homozygous in every mutant and never homozygous in a wild-type sibling.

Back to the bench

Four next steps, in order

1

Confirm linkage

Genotype about 20 mutants and 20 siblings at 3–4 chr7 markers, e.g. HERC1 Q3550H, plus markers near 22 and 33 Mb.

2

Sequence the leads

Sanger the coding exons and UTRs of tmppe, TJP1, HERC1, cog4 and thap11 in mutants.

3

Test function

Crispants or complementation crosses; compare Alcian blue cartilage staining at 4–5 dpf.

4

Sequence deeper

15× or more, paired-end, pools of 20–50 mutants and 20–50 siblings, run in sibling mode.

A causal recessive mutation is homozygous in every mutant and never in a wild-type sibling · 43

The analysis points to a region and a few leads; the bench decides. First confirm linkage cheaply: genotype a few markers across the peak in individual mutants and siblings. If every mutant is homozygous at a marker and no sibling is, the mutation is close; recombinants narrow the interval. Second, Sanger-sequence the leads in that narrower interval, including UTRs, because cog4 and thap11 have no coding change. Third, test function with crispants or complementation. Finally, for the next mutant, plan deeper paired-end sequencing of pools from the start; it resolves the interval far better than one fish per group at 3x.
