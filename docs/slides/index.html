This is the whole job in one picture. Any two zebrafish differ from the reference genome at millions of places; lab strains are very diverse. Almost all of that is harmless background. The pipeline removes what the sibling also carries, keeps changes that hit proteins, and ranks them. Even then we are left with hundreds of candidates, so the last steps are always biology and bench work: which gene fits the phenotype, and does the variant segregate with it.

These seven words are enough to read the report. Coverage is the one people underestimate: at 3x, a heterozygous variant is often seen in only one read, or none, so a single fish at low coverage can look homozygous by chance. Allele fraction is what sibling mode uses: it is simply the share of reads at that spot that carry the variant.

This is the most important genetic point in the tutorial. Siblings are the best controls because they share most of the background with the mutant. But two thirds of wild-type-looking siblings carry one copy of a recessive mutation. A carrier shows the mutation in about half its reads; a pool of siblings in about a third. If the analysis demanded zero reads in the control, it would discard the causal mutation two times out of three. What a sibling can never be is homozygous for it, because then it would be a mutant.

Around the mutation, the mutant inherited the same stretch of chromosome twice, so every variant there is homozygous.

The pipeline counts homozygous mutant variants per Mb. A tall peak shows where to look for the mutation.

Each colour is a stretch of chromosome inherited from one grandparent · 7

A mutation is never inherited alone. It travels with the piece of chromosome it sits on, and recombination only slowly breaks that piece up over generations. So in a homozygous mutant, the region around the mutation is identical on both copies, and every ordinary variant in that region also looks homozygous. A sibling carries the region on at most one copy. This is homozygosity mapping: the pipeline counts homozygous variants per megabase along the genome, and the tallest peak is where the mutation most likely sits. With few meioses the region can be several megabases long and contain dozens of genes.

This is the one setting that depends on your experiment. Absent mode is strict: the control must show no trace of the variant. That is right for an unrelated control, or for a dominant or brand-new mutation. Sibling mode is for the usual screen design: mutants versus their wild-type-looking siblings. Step one cleans each read. Library preparation adds a short synthetic adapter to both ends of every DNA fragment. If the fragment is shorter than the read length, 151 letters here, the sequencer runs past the zebrafish DNA into the adapter, and those letters would not match the genome. fastp finds and cuts them. In our data most fragments were short, so most reads were trimmed, but almost all were kept.

Step two places every read on the reference genome with bwa-mem2. Each placement comes with a mapping quality, a measure of how sure the aligner is. One scale, three questions

Base quality: is this letter wrong? Set by the sequencer.

Mapping quality: is this read in the wrong place? bwa-mem2 compares the best and second-best placement.

QUAL: is there no variant here at all? bcftools, next slides.

Phred scale, named after the first program that used it · 22

Before the next steps, a word on the numbers. Every quality score in sequencing uses the same Phred scale: the score is minus ten times the log, base ten, of the chance of being wrong. So 20 means one in a hundred, 30 one in a thousand, and every extra ten means ten times fewer errors. The pipeline uses it three times. Base quality says how likely a single letter is a sequencing error; we ignore letters below 20, and the fastp report counts the share of letters at 30 or more. Mapping quality says how likely the read is in the wrong place; bwa-mem2 derives it from how much better the best placement scores than the second best, so a read in a repeat, with two equally good places, gets zero. We ignore reads below 20. QUAL is the chance that the site is not a variant at all, which we will compute on the next math slide; we keep calls of 30 or more.

Step four finds the differences. To use all the cores, the genome is cut into 30 pieces processed in parallel. For each position, reads are stacked up, ignoring poorly placed reads and low-quality letters. Then all fish are genotyped together, which is important: for every variant we get the read counts in every fish, including fish that do not carry it. That is how we know the sibling's six reads at the tmppe position all show C. Finally the calls are tidied so that each line holds one change and insertions and deletions are written in a standard way, which the known-variant matching needs, and calls below quality 30 are dropped.

The math · Calling a genotype

Which genotype best explains the reads?

n reads, k of them show the new letter T, each letter wrong with chance ε (Q30: ε = 0.001)

P(reads | CC) = (ε/3)k (1 − ε)n−k
P(reads | CT) = (½)n
P(reads | TT) = (1 − ε)k (ε/3)n−k

CT: each read comes from either copy, a coin flip. An error gives any of 3 other letters: ε/3. bcftools weighs the three with a prior (about 1 position in 1,000 differs) and reports the most likely.

QUAL = −10 log10 P(no sample has T | reads)

tmppe, chr7:29,157,506 C>T (equal priors)

The weak spot at 3× coverage: a heterozygote shows only T in (½)n of cases, 1 in 8 with 3 reads and 1 in 32 with 5. Some “homozygous” calls are really one copy.

Simplified: bcftools also uses each letter’s own quality and all samples together · full derivation: slides 65–69 · 24

Here is how a genotype is decided. Take one position with n reads, k of them showing the new letter. For each possible genotype we ask how likely these reads would be. If the sample is CC, every T must be a sequencing error, so the chance is epsilon over three to the power k, tiny; the three is because an error can produce any of the three other letters. If it is TT, every C must be an error. If it is CT, each read comes from one of the two copies at random, like a coin flip, so any particular set of reads has chance one half to the power n. For the tmppe mutant, five T reads: CC is essentially impossible, CT has a chance of one in thirty-two, TT almost one. So the call is homozygous, with about 97 percent confidence. For the sibling, six C reads, the call is CC with about 98 percent. bcftools also adds a prior, the expectation that only about one position in a thousand differs, and uses each letter's own quality. QUAL is the Phred-scaled chance that no sample carries the variant. The key lesson: at low coverage, a heterozygote can look homozygous by chance, one time in eight with three reads.

Where do 90 and 60 percent come from? In a sample with the variant on one of its two copies, each read shows it with chance one half, like a coin flip, so the number of reads showing it follows the binomial distribution. The first column is the chance that a heterozygous mutant looks homozygous, at least 90 percent of reads: one in eight with three reads, but only one in a hundred with ten. The other two columns are the cost on the sibling side. The sibling looks normal, so it is a carrier two times in three: half the clutch are carriers and a quarter are homozygous wild type. The protein check has no p value; it is an alignment followed by counting. To line up two proteins, every pair of amino acids that sits in the same column gets a BLOSUM62 score. The score is twice the log, base two, of how much more often that pair is seen in alignments of truly related proteins than expected by chance. Identical tryptophans score eleven because tryptophan is rare and rarely replaced; isoleucine to valine scores plus three because it is a common, mild swap; proline to leucine scores minus three. Gaps cost ten to open and a half for each extra letter. The aligner tries every arrangement efficiently with dynamic programming and keeps the highest total. Then, for the variant position, we only trust the lined-up residue if at least six of the twenty neighbouring residues are identical: two unrelated sequences match only about six percent of the time. Finally the decision is plain counting across species. lgr4 isoleucine 745 is valine in all six other species, so the mutant valine is the vertebrate consensus and moves to priority 5. tmppe arginine 370 is arginine in all three species that aligned well, so it is conserved and listed first.

The Manhattan plot in the report shows one dot per variant, and its height is minus log ten of a p value. Here is where that p value comes from. At each variant we count reads in a two-by-two table: mutant or sibling, new letter or reference. The null hypothesis is that the new letter has nothing to do with which sample the read came from. Then the new-letter reads are just a random handful drawn from all the reads, and the number landing in the mutant follows the hypergeometric distribution. The p value is the chance of the mutant getting at least as many as it did. For tmppe, all five new-letter reads are in the mutant, and there is only one way out of 462 to put five reads into the mutant's five slots, so p is 0.0022 and minus log ten p is 2.66. Two warnings. First, the score is capped by depth: the same perfect pattern at fifteen-fold coverage would score 8.2, but at our three-fold coverage 99.99 percent of dots stay below 3. Second, we run about thirteen million tests without correction, so single high dots happen by chance; a Bonferroni cut-off would need 8.41, and our highest dot, inside the chromosome 7 peak, reaches 5.2. A causal region shows as a tower of many dots, which is why we rely on the peak.

The score of a 250 kb window

What to expect

Every mutant chromosome the same version: \(s \approx \varepsilon\), sequencing errors, about 1–2%.

Two different copies: \(s \approx 0.45\), the genome-wide typical value (not 0.5, because the two copies match at some sites).

A pool in which a share \(f\) of chromosomes differ: \(s \approx 0.9\,f\). One fish can only give about 0 or 0.45.

Homozygosity mapping asks one direct question in each window: does any of the mutant DNA come from a second version of this chromosome? It only uses informative sites, where the mutant carries the new letter and the sibling shows the reference letter. At those sites a reference read in the mutant cannot come from the mutant's own version, so it comes from another one, or from a sequencing error. The score is simply the reference reads divided by all mutant reads, summed over the window. In our best window, 104 of 5,632 reads, 1.8 percent, about the error rate. Where the mutant has two different copies the score is about 0.45, the typical value in the genome; not quite a half, because at some informative sites the mutant's two copies happen to carry the same letter. One diploid fish can only give about 0 or about 0.45. A pool of fish can give anything in between, roughly 0.9 times the share of chromosomes that carry another version. The best interval is the stretch around the genome's lowest point whose windows stay within two percentage points of the lowest window; the linked region is the wider stretch where most of the mutant DNA still carries one version.

\[\underbrace{P(G\mid D)}_{\text{posterior}}\;\propto\;\underbrace{P(D\mid G)}_{\text{measurement model}}\;\times\;\underbrace{P(G)}_{\text{biological prior}}\]

Measurement model

How the reads would look if the genotype were G: base errors, mapping errors, alignment errors, correlated errors.

Biological prior

How plausible G is before seeing reads: mutation rate, allele frequency, ploidy, Hardy–Weinberg.

Decision rule

\(\hat G=\arg\max_G P(G\mid D)\), then filters (QUAL ≥ 30) and our sibling-mode rules.

D = the reads at one position · G = a genotype: 0/0 (reference), 0/1 (one copy), 1/1 (two copies) · 62

Calling is not one formula but a chain. The aligner places reads, samtools sorts and cleans them, mpileup stacks them at each position and computes genotype likelihoods, and bcftools call turns those into genotypes and a site quality. Everything rests on Bayes’ rule. The likelihood, P of D given G, is the measurement model: how compatible the reads are with each genotype, given what we know about sequencing and alignment errors. The prior, P of G, is biology: how often positions differ at all. Their product, normalised, is the posterior. Then come decision rules: pick the most probable genotype, apply filters, and finally our own sibling-mode rules. Keeping these three layers apart avoids most misreadings of a VCF.

The Phred transformation shows up everywhere, but it is applied to different events. Base quality is about a single letter. Mapping quality is about the whole read being at the right place; aligners calibrate it differently, so a MAPQ from bwa-mem2 is not exactly comparable with one from another aligner. PL is not an error probability at all: it is the likelihood of each genotype relative to the best one, on the Phred scale. GQ is the chance that the chosen genotype for one sample is wrong; our run does not write it, but it can be added. QUAL is about the whole site: the chance that no sample carries any variant. One more trap: averaging Phred scores. A base at Q40 and a base at Q10 average to Q25, but their average error rate is five percent, which is Q13. The fastp and samtools reports show mean qualities, so a decent mean can hide a tail of bad bases.

Three ways a single base can mislead

Base quality \(Q_i\)

Was the letter read correctly?

\[\epsilon_i=10^{-Q_i/10}\]

Set by the sequencer from the signal of each cycle.

Ours: mpileup -Q 20 drops letters with \(\epsilon_i>0.01\)

Mapping quality \(MQ_i\)

Is the whole read at the right locus?

\[\delta_i=10^{-MQ_i/10}\]\[P(b_i\mid a)=(1-\delta_i)\,P(b_i\mid a,\text{right})+\delta_i\cdot\tfrac14\]

A misplaced read says nothing about this site: any of 4 letters.

Ours: -q 20 drops reads with \(\delta_i>0.01\)

Base alignment quality

Is the base in the right column near an indel?

\[BAQ_i=-10\log_{10}\big[1-P(A_i\mid\text{read},\text{ref})\big]\]

\(P(A_i)\) sums over all paths of a profile HMM with match, insertion and deletion states.

Ours: on by default near indels; quality used = \(\min(Q_i, BAQ_i)\)

A base can be read perfectly, from a read placed perfectly, and still sit in the wrong column. Each error source needs its own probability.

BAQ: Li 2011, Bioinformatics 27:1157 · bcftools ≥ 1.13 applies it in problem regions only (-D: everywhere) · 64

One observed letter can be wrong for three independent reasons. First, the sequencer can misread it; that is the base quality. Second, the whole read can come from somewhere else in the genome, a paralog or a repeat; that is the mapping quality. A misplaced read tells us nothing about this site, so in the mixture model it contributes a flat one quarter for every letter. Third, near an insertion or deletion the aligner may shift a base by one or two columns; that is base alignment quality, computed with a profile hidden Markov model that sums over all the ways the read could be aligned locally. mpileup takes the lower of the base quality and BAQ, then drops bases below our threshold of 20, which removes many false SNPs next to indels. Since bcftools 1.13, BAQ is only applied in regions that look problematic, to save time.

Appendix · Genotype likelihood

From one letter to all reads: \(P(D\mid G)\)

1 · One letter \(b_i\), if the true allele is \(a\):

\[P(b_i\mid a)=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon_i & b_i=a\\[2pt] \epsilon_i/3 & b_i\ne a\end{cases}\]

2 · One genotype (diploid: each copy sampled with chance ½):

\[P(b_i\mid G)=\sum_a \frac{n_a(G)}{2}\,P(b_i\mid a)\]

3 · All reads, independent given \(G\), added in log space:

\[\log_{10}P(D\mid G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_{10}P(b_i\mid G)\]

Example: 10 reads at Q30 (\(\epsilon=0.001\)): 5 A, 5 G

Why ε/3? An error is assumed equally likely to give any of the 3 other letters.

Why logs? Multiplying thousands of small numbers underflows to 0 on a computer.

\(n_a(G)\) = copies of allele \(a\) in genotype \(G\): for AG, \(n_A=n_G=1\) each · 65

Here is the measurement model step by step. For one letter, if the true allele matches, the chance of seeing it is one minus the error rate; otherwise it is the error rate divided by three, assuming an error is equally likely to produce any other letter. For a genotype, each read comes from one of the two chromosome copies, so we average over the alleles the genotype contains: a heterozygote mixes the two with weight one half. Assuming reads are independent given the genotype, the likelihood of all reads is the product, which in practice is a sum of logs. In the example, five A and five G reads at Q30: the heterozygote explains them with probability one half to the tenth, while either homozygote has to explain five reads as errors, which costs about fourteen orders of magnitude. The PL column is the same comparison on the Phred scale, which the next slides use.

When reads are not independent

\(\prod_i P(b_i\mid G)\) treats every read as a new witness. PCR duplicates, one strand, one cycle or one sequence context can repeat the same error.

The MAQ-style model in samtools sorts same-type errors by quality and discounts each further one:

\[\epsilon'_j=\epsilon_j^{\,\alpha^{\,j-1}},\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1\]

So evidence cannot grow like \(c^n\) from one artefact. Marking duplicates removes the most common cause.

Correlated-error model: Li 2011, Bioinformatics 27:2987 · our sibling-mode 90%/60% cut-offs come after calling, on AD counts · 66

A common belief is that a variant is called when the alternate share passes some threshold. That is not how bcftools works. Take nine A reads and one G read. If the G letter has quality 30, the heterozygote is about three times more likely than the homozygote, because one error at one in three thousand is less plausible than a one-half coin flip landing nine to one. If the same G has quality 20, the homozygote wins, because a one-in-three-hundred error explains it more easily. The prior on the next slides will push this further towards AA. The other assumption is independence. Twenty reads that share one artefact are not twenty witnesses. The samtools error model discounts repeated errors of the same kind: each further one counts with an exponent that shrinks. Our own sibling-mode rules, the 90 and 60 percent, are applied later to read counts; they are decision rules, not part of this model.

PL: the likelihoods our VCF actually stores

\[PL(G)=\mathrm{round}\Big[-10\big(\log_{10}L(G)-\max_H\log_{10}L(H)\big)\Big]\]

The VCF does not store raw likelihoods but PL: each genotype's log-likelihood relative to the best one, times minus ten, rounded. The best genotype is always zero, and every ten points means ten times less likely. Here is the check against our own file. Appendix · Bayes and the prior

Likelihood × prior = posterior

\[P(G\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid G)\,P(G)}{\sum_H P(D\mid H)\,P(H)}\qquad P(0/0)=(1-f)^2,\;\;P(0/1)=2f(1-f),\;\;P(1/1)=f^2\]

9 A + 1 G at Q30 (likelihood favours AG ×2.9), with Hardy–Weinberg priors at allele frequency f:

fP(AA | D)P(AG | D)

0.001 (rare)99.4%0.6%

\(\arg\max_G P(D\mid G)\) need not equal \(\arg\max_G P(G\mid D)\): weak evidence for a rare allele loses to the prior.

Genotype quality, GQ

\[GQ=-10\log_{10}\big[1-P(\hat G\mid D)\big]\]

Our run does not write GQ. A rough stand-in is the second-smallest PL (it ignores the prior):

tmppe mutant: 15 → about 3% chance the genotype is wrong. TJP1 mutant: 9 → about 13%.

Priors can also come from pedigrees, reference panels or mutation rates · GQ can be added with bcftools call -f GQ · 68

Bayes’ rule turns the likelihood into a probability by multiplying with a prior and normalising. A natural prior for a diploid is Hardy–Weinberg: with allele frequency f, two copies has probability f squared, one copy two f times one minus f. Return to nine A and one G at Q30, where the likelihood favours the heterozygote about three to one. If the allele is common, the posterior agrees: 85 percent heterozygous. If it is rare, one in a thousand, the prior wins and the posterior is 99 percent homozygous reference. That is why the most likely genotype by likelihood can differ from the most probable one. GQ is the Phred-scaled chance that the chosen genotype is wrong. Our run does not write it, but the second-smallest PL is a rough stand-in: 15 for the tmppe mutant, about a three percent chance that it is really heterozygous; 9 for the TJP1 mutant, about thirteen percent.

Appendix · bcftools call -m

First decide which alleles exist, then genotype

1 · Allele frequencies from quality-weighted evidence \(Q^x_{i,k}\), pooled over \(N\) samples:

\[f_x^{\,i}=\frac{\sum_k Q^x_{i,k}}{\sum_{y,k}Q^y_{i,k}},\qquad f_x=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_x^{\,i}\]

2 · Each allele set \(S\) (e.g. {C} or {C,T}), genotypes summed out:

\[L_S=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{x,y\in S}f_{x\mid S}\,f_{y\mid S}\,G_i(xy),\qquad P(S\mid D)\propto P(S)\,L_S\]

3 · Genotypes are then called using only the alleles in the best \(S\).

The prior: how often sites vary

\(a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\tfrac1k\approx\ln n+0.577,\quad E[\#\text{variable sites}]=\theta\,a_n\)

Ours: \(\theta=1.1\times10^{-3}\) (default -P), 2 samples = 4 copies: \(a_4=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13=1.83\), so about 0.2% of sites are expected to vary.

Site QUAL

\(QUAL=-10\log_{10}P(S=\{\text{ref}\}\mid D)\)

tmppe: QUAL 138, so \(P(\text{no variant})\approx10^{-13.8}\). High QUAL does not mean every sample’s genotype is certain.

Model: Danecek et al., samtools.github.io/bcftools/call-m.pdf · \(a_n\): Watterson’s estimator · 69

The multiallelic caller, the -m in our command, splits the problem in two. First, which alleles exist at this site at all? It estimates each allele's frequency from the quality-weighted evidence in each sample, averages over samples, and then for every candidate set of alleles computes the likelihood of all samples together, summing over the genotypes each sample could have. A prior on the allele set comes from population genetics: Watterson's harmonic number says how many variable sites to expect among n chromosome copies for a mutation rate theta. With our two diploid samples, four copies, about one site in five hundred is expected to vary. Second, once the allele set is chosen, each sample is genotyped using only those alleles. Joint calling matters because evidence is pooled: several samples with weak support can together show that an allele is real. QUAL is the Phred-scaled posterior that only the reference allele is present: 138 for tmppe, overwhelming. But QUAL is a site property; one sample's genotype can still be uncertain.

Depth sounds simple, but a VCF line can carry several versions. Raw depth counts every read over the position; filtered depth drops poorly mapped reads, duplicates and flagged reads; the allele depths count only letters that passed the base-quality filter and were assigned to an allele. They are counts, not likelihoods. The second half explains why so many of our variants are low confidence. If reads land at random, depth at any one site follows a Poisson distribution with the average coverage as its mean. At 2.8-fold, the sibling has no reads at all at six percent of sites, and four or more reads at only 31 percent. So two thirds of the time the sibling cannot be judged, whatever the variant is. At our actual variant sites it is a little better, 40 percent, because variant calling needs reads in the first place. At fifteen-fold almost every site would pass. Real data are clumpier than Poisson, so the true numbers are somewhat worse.

Appendix · Rules, not probabilities

Duplicates, indels and filters are deterministic

Duplicates

samtools markdup groups reads by a key; single-end:

\(T=(\text{chrom},\,\text{unclipped }5'\text{ pos},\,\text{strand})\)

Paired-end adds the mate’s position. The read with the highest

\(S(r)=\sum_{i:\,Q_i\ge15}Q_i\)

is kept; the others are flagged. Ours: 12.8–13.2% flagged.

Indels

Reads are compared with whole local haplotypes, summing over alignments \(A\):

\(P(r\mid H_j)=\sum_A P(r,A\mid H_j)\)

In TCACACAG, deleting any one CA gives the same sequence. bcftools norm writes the leftmost, so one event has one name, which exact matching to Ensembl needs.

Filters

Boolean partitions; no new inference. Ours:

\(\text{keep}=(QUAL\ge30)\wedge(ALT\ne{*})\)

and in sibling mode, per variant:

\(\text{specific}=(a_m\ge2)\wedge(d_m\ge3)\wedge\big(\tfrac{a_m}{d_m}\ge0.9\big)\)
\(\qquad\qquad\;\wedge\,(d_s\ge4)\wedge(a_s=0)\)

\(a\) = new-letter reads, \(d\) = reads, m = mutant, s = sibling.

Sorting and indexing are algorithms: sort is \(O(n\log n)\); a BAM index splits the genome into nested bins, so reading a region touches only the blocks that overlap it.

Specifications: samtools.github.io/hts-specs · bcftools filter semantics differ for “any sample” and “all samples” · 71

Not everything in the pipeline is statistics. Duplicate marking is a rule: reads with the same start, strand and, for pairs, the same mate position are treated as copies of one fragment, and the one with the best base qualities is kept. It is geometry, not proof that two molecules were identical. Indels are harder to model than single letters, because the read has to be compared with whole local haplotypes and many alignments are possible, especially in repeats. The same deletion in a repeat can also be written at several positions; normalisation moves it to the leftmost, so the same event always gets the same description, which our exact matching against Ensembl relies on. Filters are yes-or-no rules applied after calling. Our quality filter keeps QUAL of 30 or more, and our sibling-mode categories are likewise rules on read counts. A filter does not make any call more correct; it only chooses which calls we look at.

Appendix · Runs of homozygosity

Homozygosity mapping as a hidden Markov model

Two hidden states along the chromosome: AZ, both copies identical by descent, and HW, ordinary. With allele frequency \(f\):

\[P(D_i\mid AZ)=(1-f)\,P(D_i\mid RR)+f\,P(D_i\mid AA)\]

\[P(D_i\mid HW)=(1-f)^2P(D_i\mid RR)+2f(1-f)P(D_i\mid RA)+f^2P(D_i\mid AA)\]

Switching state is likelier over larger genetic distances. The best path (Viterbi):

\[V_i(s)=P(D_i\mid s)\,\max_{s'}\big[V_{i-1}(s')\,P(s\mid s')\big]\]

How the pipeline runs it

SNPs only. Calls with < 3 reads, or “heterozygous” on a single read of one allele, are set to missing first: one error-prone read otherwise breaks every run. Then genotype mode, each call trusted at Q30 (-G 30).

Read windows, not single runs

At 3× runs come out fragmented, so the pipeline reports the share of each 1 Mb window in runs, mutant minus sibling. Ours: chr7 27–31 Mb, mutant 92%, sibling 12%.

RR, RA, AA = reference, heterozygous, alternate · forward–backward gives per-site probabilities · Narasimhan et al. 2016 · 72

Our homozygosity peak can also be found with a proper statistical model, and the pipeline now runs one: bcftools roh. It walks along the chromosome with two hidden states. In an autozygous stretch both copies come from the same ancestor, so every site must be homozygous. In an ordinary stretch all three genotypes are possible with Hardy–Weinberg weights. The chance of switching state between two sites grows with the distance between them. The Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely sequence of states. At our three-fold coverage one detail mattered: a single error-prone read can make several neighbouring sites look heterozygous, and each of those breaks a run. So the pipeline first sets weak calls to missing, calls with fewer than three reads or heterozygous calls resting on a single read of one allele, and then runs roh on the genotype calls, trusting each at Q30. Even so, runs come out in pieces, so we read the share of each one-megabase window that lies in runs. For our mutant that gives chromosome 7, 27 to 31 megabases: 92 percent in runs in the mutant, 12 percent in the sibling.

Appendix · Summary

Six ways to misread a VCF

AD is not a likelihood

5 reads to 0 says less than PL 177,15,0, which also weighs every letter’s quality.

PL is not a posterior

It has no prior. A rare allele needs more evidence than PL alone suggests.

GQ is not QUAL

QUAL: is the site variant at all? GQ: is this sample’s genotype right?

Depth is not evidence

20 copies of one artefact are one observation, not 20.

A call is not a read count

Base, mapping and alignment quality and the prior all enter.

Filters don’t fix the model

They choose which calls to look at; they make none of them more correct.

And our own pipeline: the sibling-mode cut-offs (90% / 60%) and the homozygosity windows are decision rules on read counts, applied after calling. Slide 30 shows what they cost at 3×.

The core of it all: \(P(G\mid D)\propto P(D\mid G)\,P(G)\), with care about which reads count as independent · 73

To close the appendix, six misreadings that the mathematics helps avoid. Allele depths are raw counts; the genotype likelihoods contain more information because they weigh each letter. PL values are likelihoods without a prior, so they overstate the case for a rare allele. GQ and QUAL answer different questions. High depth is only strong evidence if the reads are independent. A genotype call is not simply a share of reads. And filters only select; they do not improve calls. Finally, be honest about our own steps: the sibling-mode cut-offs and the window counts are rules applied to read counts after calling, not part of the likelihood model, and slide 30 shows how often they can misclassify at our coverage.
