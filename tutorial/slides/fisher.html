The question: if the 5 new-letter reads had landed at random among all 11 reads, how often would the mutant get 5 or more of them?

p = Σx ≥ a C(K, x) C(N−K, n−x) / C(N, n)

Hypergeometric test, scipy.stats.hypergeom · read counts summed over all mutant and all control samples · 36

The Manhattan plot in the report shows one dot per variant, and its height is minus log ten of a p value. Here is where that p value comes from. At each variant we count reads in a two-by-two table: mutant or sibling, new letter or reference. The null hypothesis is that the new letter has nothing to do with which sample the read came from. Then the new-letter reads are just a random handful drawn from all the reads, and the number landing in the mutant follows the hypergeometric distribution. The p value is the chance of the mutant getting at least as many as it did. For tmppe, all five new-letter reads are in the mutant, and there is only one way out of 462 to put five reads into the mutant's five slots, so p is 0.0022 and minus log ten p is 2.66. Two warnings. First, the score is capped by depth: the same perfect pattern at fifteen-fold coverage would score 8.2, but at our three-fold coverage nothing gets far above 2. Second, we run fourteen million tests without correction, so single high dots happen by chance. A causal region shows as a tower of many dots, which is why we rely on the peak.
