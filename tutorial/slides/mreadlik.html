Appendix · Genotype likelihood

From one letter to all reads: \(P(D\mid G)\)

1 · One letter \(b_i\), if the true allele is \(a\):

\[P(b_i\mid a)=\begin{cases}1-\epsilon_i & b_i=a\\[2pt] \epsilon_i/3 & b_i\ne a\end{cases}\]

2 · One genotype (diploid: each copy sampled with chance ½):

\[P(b_i\mid G)=\sum_a \frac{n_a(G)}{2}\,P(b_i\mid a)\]

3 · All reads, independent given \(G\), added in log space:

\[\log_{10}P(D\mid G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_{10}P(b_i\mid G)\]

Why ε/3? An error is assumed equally likely to give any of the 3 other letters.

Why logs? Multiplying thousands of small numbers underflows to 0 on a computer.

\(n_a(G)\) = copies of allele \(a\) in genotype \(G\): for AG, \(n_A=n_G=1\) each · 65

Here is the measurement model step by step. For one letter, if the true allele matches, the chance of seeing it is one minus the error rate; otherwise it is the error rate divided by three, assuming an error is equally likely to produce any other letter. For a genotype, each read comes from one of the two chromosome copies, so we average over the alleles the genotype contains: a heterozygote mixes the two with weight one half. Assuming reads are independent given the genotype, the likelihood of all reads is the product, which in practice is a sum of logs. In the example, five A and five G reads at Q30: the heterozygote explains them with probability one half to the tenth, while either homozygote has to explain five reads as errors, which costs about fourteen orders of magnitude.
