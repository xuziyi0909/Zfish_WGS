Appendix · Runs of homozygosity

Homozygosity mapping as a hidden Markov model

Two hidden states along the chromosome: AZ, both copies identical by descent, and HW, ordinary. With allele frequency \(f\):

\[P(D_i\mid AZ)=(1-f)\,P(D_i\mid RR)+f\,P(D_i\mid AA)\]

\[P(D_i\mid HW)=(1-f)^2P(D_i\mid RR)+2f(1-f)P(D_i\mid RA)+f^2P(D_i\mid AA)\]

Switching state is likelier over larger genetic distances. The best path (Viterbi):

\[V_i(s)=P(D_i\mid s)\,\max_{s'}\big[V_{i-1}(s')\,P(s\mid s')\big]\]

What our pipeline does instead

Counts hard homozygous calls per 1 Mb window (slide 37). Simple and robust, but each call counts fully, even with 3 reads.

What the HMM adds

It uses the PL values, so a 3-read “homozygous” site counts less than a 10-read one, and it gives each site a probability of lying in a run. bcftools roh implements it; it is not in the pipeline yet.

RR, RA, AA = reference, heterozygous, alternate · forward–backward gives per-site probabilities · Narasimhan et al. 2016 · 63

Our homozygosity peak can also be found with a proper statistical model. bcftools roh walks along the chromosome with two hidden states. In an autozygous stretch, both copies come from the same ancestor, so every site must be homozygous: the reads are explained by reference-reference or alternate-alternate only. In an ordinary stretch, all three genotypes are possible with Hardy–Weinberg weights. The chance of switching state between two sites grows with the genetic distance between them. The Viterbi algorithm finds the most likely sequence of states, and the forward-backward algorithm gives each site a probability of lying in a run. The advantage over our window count is that the HMM works with the genotype likelihoods, so a homozygous call from three reads counts less than one from ten. It is not part of the pipeline yet; it would be a natural addition for low-coverage data.
